The question that started it

Most of my recent thinking about AI and design started with a relatively simple problem:

A contributor had to be able to:

What happens when the direction of the workflow is reversed?

Traditionally, digital product design moves from design into development. A designer creates screens, components and specifications, and eventually those decisions become code.

But modern AI-assisted development changes the equation.

Working interfaces can now be generated, modified and iterated extremely quickly. That led me to a more fundamental question:

If the product can now be generated from code, where does the design source of truth actually live?

Figma is excellent at representing design and collaboration. Code is excellent at representing behaviour. Documentation explains decisions. Analytics describes what happens in production.

But none of these, individually, represents the complete design intelligence of a product.

That became the starting point for a broader exploration.

From Design System to Knowledge System

I began exploring the idea that a design system should not simply contain colours, components and tokens.

It should contain the knowledge behind the decisions.

Patterns, behaviours, constraints, relationships, accessibility considerations, product rules, user insights, design rationale and implementation knowledge all contribute to the way a product behaves.

This shifted the mental model from:

Design System → Components → Screens

towards: Knowledge → Intelligence → Orchestration → Design → Product

The result was the concept of a Design Knowledge Operating System.

Rather than treating design knowledge as something stored in individual files, the system treats it as an evolving organisational asset that can be structured, connected, reused and continuously improved.

A new source of truth

The central idea is not to replace Figma, code or existing design tools.

It is to connect them.

The source of truth becomes less about a single application and more about a shared layer of design intelligence sitting between knowledge, design and implementation.

That layer can inform different outputs depending on the task.

  • A designer might need a user journey.
  • A developer might need implementation guidance.
  • An AI agent might need component constraints.
  • A product team might need the reasoning behind a particular interaction.

The underlying knowledge remains connected, while the surface changes according to the context.

This creates a more resilient model of design where the knowledge survives the individual artefact.

From orchestration to collaboration

As the concept developed, it became clear that a single AI assistant was not enough.

Different design problems require different forms of reasoning.

Research, product strategy, UX, interaction design, design systems, content, accessibility, implementation and quality assurance each have different concerns.

I therefore began exploring a layered orchestration model in which specialised capabilities can contribute to a larger workflow rather than attempting to make one model responsible for everything.

At a high level, the process becomes:

Understand → Decompose → Reason → Create → Validate → Learn

The orchestration layer determines what needs to happen, which knowledge is relevant, which capabilities should contribute, and how the resulting work feeds back into the wider system.

The intention is not autonomous design for its own sake.

It is controlled collaboration between human expertise, structured knowledge and AI capabilities.

The telemetry loop

The next question was what happens after the design has been delivered.

Traditional design workflows often end when a screen is approved and handed over.

Digital products don't.

Once something is live, users interact with it, behaviours emerge, problems are discovered and new information becomes available.

That creates another opportunity.

Instead of a linear workflow:

Design → Build → Release

the model becomes a continuous loop:

Design → Build → Observe → Learn → Refine

Telemetry, analytics, testing and qualitative feedback can become inputs into the knowledge layer.

This creates the possibility of a system where design knowledge is not simply documented once, but progressively refined through real-world evidence.

The result is a feedback loop between intention and behaviour.

Designing across the layers

What became particularly interesting was the relationship between the different layers of the product.

A change to a component can affect an interface.

An interface can affect a user journey.

A journey can expose a product problem.

A product decision can require a change to the underlying design system.

Rather than treating these as isolated activities, the operating-system approach considers their relationships.

This creates a more connected workflow across:

Product → UX → UI → Design System → Code → Runtime → Feedback

AI becomes the connective tissue between these layers, while people remain responsible for judgement, direction and accountability.

What I am exploring

This work is still an evolving body of thinking rather than a finished product.

The interesting outcome for me has been the shift in perspective.

I originally wanted to understand how AI could help move from working code back into design.

That question eventually became:

What if the real source of truth isn't the design file at all?

It could be the knowledge surrounding the product, its relationships, its decisions and the evidence continually generated by its use.

That leads towards a different kind of design system.

Not a library of components.

Not simply a collection of documentation.

But an evolving knowledge and intelligence layer for digital product design.

The opportunity I see is a future where design can move fluidly between human judgement, structured knowledge, AI reasoning, working code and real-world product behaviour, without losing the context that connects them.

That is the direction behind my exploration of the Design Knowledge Operating System.
